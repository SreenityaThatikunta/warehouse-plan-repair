\section{Problem statement}
A team of robots operates in a warehouse whose floor is modelled as a grid of square cells. At
each time step, every robot either moves to an adjacent cell (up, down, left or right) or waits.
Each robot is assigned \textbf{three tasks}, each consisting of picking up an item next to a shelf
and delivering it to a delivery station. After its last task, the robot returns to its own parking
cell (its \emph{dock}). Two robots may never occupy the same cell at the same time, and two robots
may never exchange cells within a single time step.

The robots first compute collision-free paths. While they execute these paths, three kinds of
\textbf{disruption} can occur:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item a robot \textbf{breaks down}, for 5--20 steps or permanently;
  \item a cell becomes \textbf{blocked}, for 10--40 steps or permanently;
  \item a robot receives an \textbf{emergency task}, which takes priority over its other tasks and
        has a delivery deadline.
\end{itemize}
After a disruption, the robots must \textbf{repair} their plans. The assignment requires that the
affected robots resolve the situation by communicating with nearby robots, that the multi-agent
planner is not re-run from scratch, and that the number of robots whose plans change is kept as
small as possible.

\textbf{Environment.} The warehouse map measures $18\times33$ cells. It contains 25 shelf blocks
separated by one-cell-wide aisles, 150 pickup cells adjacent to the shelves, 14 delivery stations
on the side walls and 58 docks on the top and bottom walls (see Figure~\ref{fig:demo}).

\section{Methodology}
\subsection*{Initial planning}
The initial plan is computed with \textbf{prioritized planning using Space-Time A*}. Robots are
planned sequentially, with the robot that has the longest route planned first. Space-Time A* is
the A* shortest-path algorithm applied to positions and time together, so a planned path may also
include waiting. Each completed plan is recorded in a shared \textbf{reservation table}, which marks
the cells and moves a robot will use at each time step. Every subsequent robot plans around the
existing reservations, which guarantees that the resulting plans are collision-free.

\subsection*{Plan repair}
When a disruption occurs, only the robots whose plans have become invalid are repaired, one at a
time. Each robot proceeds through a sequence of repair tiers, ordered from the least to the most
disruptive for other robots (Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}), and stops at the first tier that
produces an acceptable plan.

\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item \textbf{Individual replanning (Tier 1).} The robot replans its own path while treating
        all other plans as fixed. The new plan is accepted if it adds at most 4 steps. Only one
        plan changes.
  \item \textbf{Negotiation with neighbours (Tiers 2--3).} The robot identifies the nearby robots
        that obstruct its path and asks small groups of them (one, two, then three robots) to
        replan jointly with it, first within 6 cells and then within 12 cells. A robot that is
        asked to move but is itself obstructed may in turn ask its own neighbours, up to two
        levels deep.
  \item \textbf{Local group replanning (Tier 3b).} For urgent cases only (an emergency robot, or a
        robot with no feasible path), the robot and up to 8 nearby robots are replanned together.
  \item \textbf{Detour or wait (Tier 4).} Otherwise, the robot accepts its longer individual
        detour. If no path exists at all, it waits in place and retries at the next step. After
        waiting for 10 steps, it extends its search to robots further away.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Acceptance criterion.} Each candidate repair is assigned a score:
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.86\linewidth}{\centering \textbf{score} = extra steps of all robots involved \;+\; 3 per additional robot whose plan changes\\ \;+\; 10 if an emergency deadline is missed}}
\end{center}
A lower score is better. A joint repair with neighbours is used only if its score is lower than
that of individual replanning. The penalty of 3 per additional robot implements the requirement
to minimise the number of altered plans: involving another robot is worthwhile only if it saves
more than 3 steps.

\subsection*{Methods compared}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \texttt{local}: the proposed method described above.
  \item \texttt{local-flat}: our initial version, without multi-level negotiation, Tier 3b or
        emergency deadlines.
  \item \texttt{solo}: Tier 1 only, without communication, to measure the benefit of negotiation.
  \item \texttt{full}: replans every robot from scratch after each disruption. This is not
        permitted for repair by the assignment and serves only as a reference.
\end{itemize}

\summary{robots are planned sequentially and record their paths in a shared reservation table.
After a disruption, each affected robot applies the least disruptive repair first and involves
other robots only when doing so reduces the total delay.}

\section{Experiments and results}
\textbf{Experimental setup.} Every configuration was run with \textbf{10 random seeds}, and all
four methods were evaluated on identical warehouses and disruption sequences. By default, each run
uses an obstacle density of 3\%, two breakdowns and two emergencies. We varied the
\textbf{number of robots} (5 to 50) and the \textbf{obstacle density} (0\% to 15\%), and ran
additional experiments with exactly \textbf{one disruption} per run to measure the number of plans
altered by a single disruption. In total, 1{,}700 runs were performed. \textbf{No collisions
occurred in any run}, and all tasks were completed in every run except one \texttt{solo} run
(Section~\ref{sec:fail}).

\textbf{Main findings.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item \textbf{Few plans are altered.} The proposed method alters on average about one robot
        beyond those directly affected by the disruption, whereas replanning from scratch alters
        between 9 and 39 robots per disruption.
  \item \textbf{Replanning from scratch is not faster.} With 50 robots, its total time is higher
        than that of the proposed method (7201 versus 6958 steps), because it reassigns the paths
        of all robots, including those that were not affected.
  \item \textbf{Plans altered by a single disruption.} A temporary breakdown alters 2.2 plans (the
        robots it directly obstructs), a temporary blockage 3.1 and a permanent breakdown 13.5,
        since the broken robot's tasks are reassigned to other robots. Replanning from scratch
        alters 22 to 29 plans (Figure~\ref{fig:single}, left).
  \item \textbf{The benefit of negotiation grows with fleet size.} With 50 robots, individual
        replanning (\texttt{solo}) requires 131 more steps than the proposed method.
  \item \textbf{Improvement over the initial version.} Compared with \texttt{local-flat}, the
        final method reduces total time by 7 to 19 steps with 30 to 50 robots and meets more
        emergency deadlines (35\% versus 15\% with 50 robots), while altering a similar number of
        plans. Its repair time is approximately twice as long.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Number of robots.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item Total time increases approximately linearly, by about 140 steps per additional robot,
        since each robot adds three tasks.
  \item The time lost to disruptions increases faster, because a more crowded floor offers fewer
        detours: from 108 to 377 extra steps with the proposed method, compared with up to 620
        extra steps when replanning from scratch.
  \item Up to 20 robots, all methods perform similarly. From 30 robots onwards, the proposed
        method achieves the lowest total time, requiring 37, 107 and 243 fewer steps than
        replanning from scratch with 30, 40 and 50 robots respectively.
  \item The number of altered plans grows slowly with the proposed method (0.9 to 5.9), but almost
        in proportion to the fleet size when replanning from scratch (1.4 to 39).
  \item The cost is computation time: repair takes 3\,ms with 5 robots and 2.3\,s with 50 robots,
        since more neighbours lead to more candidate groups.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Obstacle density.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item Increasing the density from 0\% to 15\% increases the number of disruptions per run from 4
        to 45 and the total time of the proposed method by 14\% (from 2565 to 2925). Each
        additional disruption costs approximately 9 steps.
  \item The number of altered plans per disruption remains nearly constant (2.1 to 2.7), because a
        blocked cell affects only the few robots about to traverse it.
  \item At low densities, all methods are within about 10 steps of each other. From 10\% onwards,
        the proposed method saves 45 to 72 steps compared with replanning from scratch, and at
        15\% replanning from scratch becomes considerably slower (1.7\,s versus 0.3\,s per
        disruption).
\end{itemize}

\section{Settings in which the agents fail}
\label{sec:fail}
In the main experiments, failures are almost absent. This is partly because the scenario generator
never produces unsolvable situations; for example, it never blocks a cell that would make a goal
unreachable. To identify the conditions under which the robots fail, we performed a
\textbf{stress study} of 760 runs in which this safeguard was disabled and the environment was made
more demanding. A run is considered to have \textbf{failed} if at least one task is never
completed. Each robot that becomes stuck is classified as follows:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{goal unreachable}: its goal can no longer be reached, so the task has become
        impossible;
  \item \textbf{deadlocked}: its goal is still reachable, but the robot is unable to proceed.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{1. Unreachable goals.} A permanently blocked cell, or a robot that breaks down permanently
in a one-cell-wide aisle, can isolate a pickup cell or a delivery station
(Figure~\ref{fig:unreach}). Such tasks cannot be completed by any method, so all methods fail
similarly. Task completion decreases from 97\% to 27\% as the density increases to 20\%. Even a
single permanent breakdown causes 2 to 4 out of 10 runs to fail.

\textbf{2. Secondary deadlocks.} A robot whose goal has become unreachable does not abandon its
task. It remains in its cell indefinitely, and this cell may be the only passage for other robots.
At 10\% density, 2.3 robots per run have an unreachable goal, while a further 3.1 robots become
deadlocked behind them. More than half of the stuck robots are therefore caused by the robots'
response rather than by the impossibility of the task itself. The proposed method is nevertheless
more robust than replanning from scratch in this setting (81\% versus 71\% of tasks completed).

\textbf{3. Propagation of waiting on crowded maps (main weakness).} On the small map with 40
robots, 3 of 10 runs fail with nearly the entire fleet stuck. In one case, a temporary breakdown
obstructs 6 robots, which begin to wait. Robots whose plans pass through the cell of a waiting
robot then also wait, until all 40 robots are waiting for one another and only 1 of 122 tasks is
completed. The repair groups (at most 4 or 8 robots) are too small to resolve this situation,
whereas replanning from scratch resolves it. The effect depends strongly on the specific instance:
with 44 robots, no run failed.

\textbf{4. Other settings.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Very high obstacle density:} at 30\% density, with all tasks still solvable, 1 of
        10 runs results in a deadlock.
  \item \textbf{No communication:} without negotiation (\texttt{solo}), all 49 remaining robots
        became stuck after a single permanent breakdown with 50 robots (Figure~\ref{fig:gridlock}),
        and 5 of 10 runs failed on the small crowded map. The proposed method completed both.
  \item \textbf{Emergency deadlines:} in crowded warehouses, only 5--35\% of emergency deliveries
        meet their deadline, although all of them are eventually completed.
\end{itemize}
The robots did \textbf{not} fail with a reduced communication range (a range of 1 cell increases
total time by only 1\%, because a robot that has waited for 10 steps extends its search), nor in
any solvable configuration with up to 50 robots and 20\% obstacle density.

(3) waiting propagates through a highly crowded map, (4) the obstacle density is very high, or
(5) the robots cannot communicate.}

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Incompleteness of prioritized planning:} sequential planning may fail to find a
        plan even when one exists.
  \item \textbf{No task abandonment:} a robot whose goal becomes unreachable waits indefinitely
        and may obstruct other robots.
  \item \textbf{Propagation of waiting:} on crowded maps, waiting can spread across the fleet;
        replanning a larger region when many robots are waiting would address this.
  \item \textbf{Soft emergency deadlines:} a missed deadline only adds a penalty, so most deadlines
        are missed in crowded warehouses.
  \item \textbf{Computation time:} repair takes about 2 seconds per disruption with 50 robots in
        Python.
  \item \textbf{Idealised communication:} messages are assumed to be delivered instantly and
        without loss.
\end{itemize}
