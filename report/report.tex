\begin{abstract}
Robots in a grid warehouse carry items from pickup cells to delivery stations along
collision-free paths from prioritized Space-Time A* (ST-A*). During execution, robots break
down, cells become blocked and robots receive urgent tasks. We repair the joint plan
\emph{locally}: affected robots negotiate over a range-limited message bus with the smallest
useful set of neighbours, and the global planner is never re-run. Over 1{,}700 runs (5--50
robots, 0--15\% dynamic obstacle density) there were no collisions, and local repair changed
5--7$\times$ fewer plans per disruption than replanning from scratch, at equal or lower total
time. A stress study of 760 more runs identifies where the agents \emph{fail} to finish their
tasks: disruptions that cut goals off, robots stuck behind them, cascading holds on crowded
maps, very high obstacle density, and the absence of negotiation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and environment}
$n$ robots move on a 4-connected grid. In each step a robot moves to a neighbouring free cell
or waits. Vertex conflicts (two robots in one cell) and edge conflicts (swaps) are forbidden.
Each robot has 3 pickup$\to$delivery tasks and then returns to its own dock. The map is an
$18\times33$ Kiva-style warehouse: $5\times5$ shelf blocks of $2\times5$ cells separated by
one-cell aisles. It has 344 free cells, 150 pickup cells, 14 stations and 58 dead-end docks.

There are three kinds of disruption: \textbf{breakdowns} (5--20 steps, or permanent; a
permanently broken robot's tasks are auctioned to robots in range),
\textbf{blockages} (10--40 steps, or permanent) and \textbf{emergency tasks} (a new
pickup--delivery task that goes first, with a deadline of the ideal delivery time + 3 steps).
Events are generated from the seed, so all strategies face the same schedule. The
\emph{dynamic obstacle density} $\rho$ is the number of blockages divided by the number of
free cells. By default a filter keeps every instance solvable (Section~\ref{sec:fail} turns it
off). Robots within Manhattan distance $\R=6$ exchange messages (ALERT, REQUEST, PROPOSE,
COMMIT, \dots), and every message is counted.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Initial plan.} Prioritized planning: robots are planned one at a time (longest task
chain first) with ST-A* over states $(x,y,\text{goal index},t)$. Each path is written into a
space-time reservation table (vertex, edge and parking reservations), and later robots plan
around it.

\textbf{Local repair.} When a disruption hits, the robots whose plans it invalidates are
repaired one at a time, trying the options that change the fewest plans first
(Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}). For robot $a$, let $\ell^\ast$ be its robot-free path and
$d(p)=|p|-|\ell^\ast|$ the delay of a path $p$. A coalition $S$ of neighbours that replan
jointly with $a$ (with $a$ planned first) is scored by
\begin{equation}
  \text{score}(S)= d(p_a)+\textstyle\sum_{m\in S}\Delta_m + \lambda|S| + \text{DL}(p_a),
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_m$ is member $m$'s added completion time, $\lambda=3$ is the price of changing
one more robot's plan, and DL adds a penalty of 10 if an emergency deadline is missed. A
coalition is accepted only if it scores better than going solo.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Tier 1, solo:} replan $a$ alone with all other plans fixed. Accept if the delay
        is at most $\delta=4$ (0 for emergency robots).
  \item \textbf{Tiers 2--3, negotiation} within $\R$, then $2\R$. Candidates are the movable
        robots that block $\ell^\ast$. Coalitions of size 1, 2, 3 are tried in order, and the
        search stops at the first size that beats going solo. A member that cannot dodge may
        recruit its own blockers (a \emph{chain}, at most 2 hops and 6 robots), scored as one tree.
  \item \textbf{Tier 3b, local group replan:} for urgent robots (emergency, or no solo path),
        prioritized ST-A* for the at most 8 robots within $2\R$ that cross $a$'s path.
  \item \textbf{Tier 4, hold:} otherwise $a$ takes its solo detour or, if none exists, waits in
        place and retries every step. After 10 steps on hold its search radius doubles
        (expanding ring).
\end{itemize}

We compare \textbf{local} (the method above), \textbf{local-flat} (our first version, without
chains, Tier 3b or deadlines), \textbf{solo} (Tier 1 only, an ablation) and \textbf{full}, which
re-runs prioritized ST-A* for every robot after every disruption. \textbf{full} is exactly what
the assignment forbids, and serves as a reference.

\section{Experiments and results}
Each setting was run for 10 seeds with every strategy on identical instances. Defaults are 3\%
density (20\% of blockages permanent), 2 breakdowns and 2 emergencies per run. We varied the
number of robots (5--50), the density (0--15\% with 20 robots), the alter penalty
($\lambda=0$--12), and ran single-disruption runs of each type.
\textbf{Altered} counts the robots whose future plan changed because of a disruption (including
cascades). Every executed step is checked for collisions. All 1{,}700 runs had zero collisions,
and all completed every task except one \texttt{solo} run (Section~\ref{sec:fail}).

\textbf{Plans changed.} Local repair changes about one robot beyond those a disruption hits
directly. Full replanning changes 9--39 robots per disruption, nearly the whole fleet, and is
not faster: at 50 robots its SoC is \emph{worse} (7201 vs 6958), because replanning everyone
reshuffles priorities and delays robots that were not affected. In single-disruption runs, a
temporary breakdown changes 2.2 plans (exactly the robots hit), a temporary blockage 3.1, and a
permanent breakdown 13.5 (mostly the robots hit directly plus the winners of its tasks), against
22--29 for full replanning.

\textbf{Total time and scaling.} \texttt{local} has the lowest or near-lowest SoC at every
size. Negotiation matters more as the fleet grows: \texttt{solo} needs 131 more steps at 50
robots. Compared with \texttt{local-flat}, the final version lowers SoC by 7--19 steps at 30--50
robots and makes more emergencies on time (35\% vs 15\% at 50 robots), changing about as many
plans. The cost is about twice the repair time, which reaches 2.3\,s per disruption at 50 robots.
From 0\% to 15\% density, the extra time grows from 143 to 503 steps while plans changed per
disruption stay between 2.1 and 2.7. Raising $\lambda$ from 0 to 12 trades a few extra steps
for fewer altered robots; at 40 robots our default $\lambda=3$ beats the first version at any
$\lambda$ on both measures.

\section{Settings where agents fail}
\label{sec:fail}
In the main sweeps robots rarely fail, partly because the generator filters out disruptions that
make a task impossible. A \textbf{stress study} (760 runs, 10 seeds, all strategies) switches
the filter off and pushes the environment further. A run \emph{fails} if a task is undone when
it ends (every robot done, or nothing moved for 150 steps). Each stuck robot is labelled
\emph{goal cut off} (its goal is unreachable, so the instance is unsolvable) or
\emph{deadlocked} (every goal reachable, but stuck).

\textbf{Goals cut off.} With the filter off, a permanent blockage or a robot that breaks down
for good can seal off a pickup cell, a station or an aisle segment (Figure~\ref{fig:unreach}).
No repair can finish those tasks, so all strategies fail alike: completion drops from 97\% to
27\% as density rises to 20\%. A single permanent breakdown already makes 2--4 of 10 runs
fail, because a dead robot in a one-cell aisle is a wall.

\textbf{Knock-on deadlock.} A robot whose goal is cut off never gives up its task and holds its
cell forever, which can block other robots' only route. At 10\% density, 2.3 robots per run are
cut off and another 3.1 are deadlocked behind them, so more than half of the failures come from
the agents' reaction rather than the instance. Local repair suffers less than full replanning
here (81\% vs 71\% completed).

\textbf{Hold cascade on crowded maps (our main weakness).} On a small map with 40 robots, 3 of
10 \texttt{local} runs fail, each with almost the whole fleet stuck. In one, a \emph{temporary}
breakdown blocks 6 robots, which hold. Every robot planned through a held cell then holds too,
until all 40 robots wait on each other and only 1 of 122 tasks is delivered. Coalitions (at most
4 robots) and Tier 3b (at most 8) are too small to untangle this, while full replanning solves the
same instance. The effect depends on the instance: at 44 robots no run failed.

\textbf{Other settings.} At 30\% density (all tasks solvable), 1 of 10 runs deadlocks. Without
negotiation (\texttt{solo}), all 49 remaining robots gridlock after one permanent breakdown with
50 robots, and 5 of 10 runs fail on the crowded map with 44 robots; \texttt{local} solves both.
Emergency deadlines are met in only 5--35\% of cases in dense fleets, though the tasks are
eventually delivered. The agents did \emph{not} fail with a short communication radius
($\R=1$ costs only 1\% more time, since holding robots widen their search), nor in any
solvable instance on the main map with up to 50 robots and 20\% density.

\section{Limitations}
Prioritized planning is incomplete, so neither the initial plan nor joint replans are guaranteed
to succeed. Robots never release a task whose goal is cut off. Hold cascades on crowded maps need
an escalation to a larger regional replan. Emergency deadlines are soft. Repair takes seconds at
50 robots in Python. Communication is assumed instant and lossless.
